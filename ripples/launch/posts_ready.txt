## LinkedIn (personal; attach the launch video, Desktop/Ripple/ripple-launch.mp4, 1600x900, 33 s. Fallback: launch/stills/ripple-tiger-king-1600x900.png)

I've been fascinated by the butterfly effect for as long as I can remember. Not the movie version. The quiet version: the sense that the world we live in rests on a long chain of small outputs, and that if one had come out a little differently, almost nothing downstream would look the same.

So I built a thing to follow the thread, and to be honest about how sure we can be at each step.

It's called Ripple. You throw a stone (a show, a film, a disaster, a discovery) into a pond. The ripples spread out in time; each one is something that changed afterward, placed where it began. Every link is graded: measured against data with a placebo test, timed in the right order, reported by a source, or just plausible. Some are busted, because the "effect" started before its "cause".

The one it opens on: Tiger King. Netflix, March 2020. Within weeks, attention to exotic pets and the wildlife trade ran several times its normal level. In December 2022, the Big Cat Public Safety Act became law. In July 2022 a congressman cited the show on the House floor, in the debate on that bill; the engine found the sentence in the Congressional Record. The arrow in the title is drawn from the weakest link on the way: reported. A source makes the link; nobody measured it. Under it, one sentence I think every story like this owes you: it wasn't the only reason. Versions of the bill had been introduced in every Congress since 2012 and campaigned for by animal-welfare groups for years.

Try Sputnik: four lasting marks in a year (ARPA, the Space Act, NASA, the Education Act) and ARPANET twelve years after Sputnik, each law linked to the statute itself. And look at the Engine leads tab: what the engine found on its own, each lead read against its source before it ships, with the sentence that makes each link. A few it found: Disney's one-year loan of Bambi is why the Forest Service created Smokey Bear, a 1955 heist film named a family of antibiotics, and YouTube's co-founder credits the 2004 Super Bowl halftime show with the idea for the site. Try Prohibition, where every popular "catalyst" for Gen Z drinking less turns out to have arrived decades after the decline began. Order is a strict judge.

Ripple is a v1 and an honest one. Timing shows order, not proof of cause. The tests, the misses and the methods are published alongside it, and so is the sentence about what else was going on.

I'd love to know which stone you'd throw.

First comment (post within a minute of submitting):

Ripple takes an event and looks forward in time for what changed downstream. Each link is graded by what backs it, strongest first:

- Measured: the data shows it. A series (Wikipedia views, baby-name counts, official statistics) jumped more than at 95% of other dates in its own history, and the jump came after the step before it.
- Timed: the order is right, but the jump wasn't tested against chance.
- Reported: a source makes the link, like a member of Congress naming the show in the debate on the bill.
- Plausible: both things happened, but nothing yet ties them together.
- Busted: it started before its supposed cause, or the data contradicts it.

Order can only falsify, so nothing here is proof of cause. The arrow in each title is drawn from the weakest link.

The linked example: Tiger King (March 2020), a member of Congress citing it on the House floor (July 2022), the Big Cat Public Safety Act becoming law (December 2022).

The engine finds candidate links in Wikipedia, Hansard and the Congressional Record, and US baby-name counts. The recent studies were pre-registered and several missed their bars; the misses are in the repo.

Weak spot: most pre-2015 links rest on sources, not data, because daily Wikipedia views start in 2015.

One HTML file, SVG and WebGL, Python on GitHub Actions, GitHub Pages.
Code and results: https://github.com/bsunter93/Ben-Sunters-Website/tree/main/ripples
Methods: https://bensunter.com/ripples/methods/

Pushback on the statistics framing is welcome.

Why it might be useful at a desk: the headline arrow is drawn from the weakest link, so the page cannot promise more than its worst card; 23 US statutes link to the law itself on govinfo, every measured card carries the run date and checker commit, and every hand-built story has a line naming what else was going on. The record of what it got wrong is published next to what it got right.

A story to test it on: Tiger King → the Big Cat Public Safety Act, where the honest version includes a bill introduced in every Congress since 2012.
