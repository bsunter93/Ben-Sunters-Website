## 1. Aisha, 31, data journalist (laptop)

Spent 20 min in Ripple (the "Tiger King → the Big Cat Act" demo) to see if it's citable for the "did Tiger King change the law" piece. Short version: the *method* is more honest than the *headline*, and I'd cite the method and not the headline.

What they do: five grades, "Measured / Timed / Reported / Plausible / Busted." The methods panel says "Measured: moved beyond normal in the data, after the step before. Timed: rose in the right order, not tested against chance." The placebo test is only explained on the card itself, under "How sure?": "We counted daily Wikipedia views of 'Greater Wynnewood Exotic Animal Park'. From Mar 23, 2020 they ran about 1280× the normal level. We then ran the same test at many random dates in the page's own past: a jump this big showed up at only 1.3% of them (p = 0.013)… This shows attention, not what people did next." That last sentence is the right disclaimer and I'd quote it.

But two things about the numbers. "Random dates" isn't what the code does: it's a fixed grid, every 14th day of the page's history, and p = (1+hits)/(1+N). For Big Cat Rescue, p = 0.01 means zero hits out of roughly 99 windows — it's the floor of the test, not "1% of them." The card never says N (just "many"). I'd report "no placebo window in the page's history matched it" rather than "p = 0.01." And "normal" is undefined on the face (it's a 68-views/day baseline median, buried in the data).

The headline is the problem. "Tiger King → the Big Cat Act" and "The mark it left: Big Cat Act becomes law" — but their own card rates the law Plausible: "The show is one of several forces behind the law; this map shows timing, not cause," and the House-vote card admits "the bill was introduced before the show." Zero measured links between the show and the statute. The two Measured cards are Wikipedia attention to a zoo and a sanctuary, three and four days after release. That's a story about attention, not law. Sourcing on the law card: a Born Free USA advocacy blog, not congress.gov. The USDA license suspension (flagged as a "lasting mark") has no link at all.

The chart ("The spikes, aligned," log y, each series at its own step) is a decent idea, but I can't hand it to the desk as is: the series colors are the same green and lilac the legend 30px below uses for "Measured" and "Timed," so Big Cat Rescue (Measured) is drawn in Timed-lilac and its card gets a lilac stripe. The y-axis runs to 10,000× for a 1,266× peak, the chart's peak (1,266×) disagrees with the card (1280×), and the spikes visibly rise *before* "the step" line because the step is the algorithm's detected onset, not the premiere. No N, no interval, no baseline definition.

What's good: the Busted cards. "Claim: The Jungle got the pure-food bill through the Senate" → "the Senate passed it five days before the book came out… Busted as the origin, plausible as the finisher." That's exactly how I'd want it stated. "Not linked yet" in grey italics is honest; I'd just want fewer of them (Liebeck: 7 of 10 cards).

Would I link it? Not as a source for "the show changed the law" — it says the opposite in its own fine print. I might link the Busted/Plausible reasoning as "a timing check." Rating: 5/10. Rigorous disclaimers, honest fail states, but the headline, the chart colors and the p-value phrasing would each get a query from you.

## 2. Tom, 67, retired Navy (iPad)

Subject: That Sputnik thing

Kath,

I looked at the link. Took me a minute. It opens on a dark blue screen that says "Throw a stone. See what it changed." with a yellow button, "Throw a stone," and a little link underneath, "Just look around." I understood the idea before I hit the button, the paragraph under the headline spells it out: "A show, a film, a disaster, a discovery. Its ripples spread out in time, each one rated for how sure we are, until something sticks: a law, an institution, a change in how people live." That's a fair description of what it is.

The stone drops into a pond and rings go out. It's more of a drop than a throw, but fine. The first story it gives you is "Tiger King → the Big Cat Act." I never watched that show and don't intend to, so I went looking for yours. "Change story" is a button top right. Tap that, and the Sputnik one is sitting right there in the list, second row. Two taps. If you type "sputnik" in the search box instead, the first thing it offers is "Sputnik → GPS," which is a different story, so I hit the wrong one once. Tell your builder friend: two Sputniks, put the big one first.

The Sputnik story is good. I checked it against what I know. Launch Oct 4, 1957, right. ARPA Feb 7, 1958, and they give the directive, "Department of Defense Directive 5105.15," and the appropriation, "Pub. L. 85-325, February 12, 1958." Space Act "Pub. L. 85-568, 72 Stat. 426, signed July 29, 1958." NDEA "Pub. L. 85-864, 72 Stat. 1580, signed September 2, 1958." NASA opens Oct 1, 1958. Every one of those is correct, and they give the public law number, which is more than most TV documentaries bother with. One nit: the last card says ARPANET's first message went "between UCLA and Stanford." Their own fine print says "UCLA to SRI." SRI is Stanford Research Institute, in Menlo Park, not the university. Small thing, but if you're going to put document numbers on the card, get the names right too.

I liked that they're honest about what they don't know. Every card has "How sure?" and on these it says "We did not measure this ourselves… Treat it as reliable reporting, not a measurement." Three Mile Island has a card struck through, "Claim: Three Mile Island stopped US reactor orders," marked "Busted," and the details say "Orders had fallen to nothing before the accident, on cost overruns, interest rates and flat demand after 1973. The accident kept them at nothing. Busted as the start; plausible as the lock." That's exactly right and I've argued it at the VFW. Same with the Exxon Valdez, they say the trust fund was created in 1986, "but Congress never authorized its tax or its use," and the spill "made it real." Good.

Complaints. The "Step" button is supposed to stop at each card, and it does, until the last one: it skips right past the ARPANET card and runs to "Replay." The sources are nearly all Wikipedia, and several say "not linked yet," which for a page built on document numbers is a letdown. Type is small; the slider years and chart labels are 10 or 11 point and I needed the glasses. And the little labels on the pond pile on top of each other by the end.

I'd give it a 7 out of 10. The history is solid and they show their work, which is rare. The polish isn't there yet.

Love, Dad

Okay so I opened the Columbine one on the bus. First thing you get is this dark screen, "Throw a stone. See what it changed." and a big orange button that says Throw a stone.
