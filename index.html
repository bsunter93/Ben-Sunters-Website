Career Ops

Built 2026 · Open source

Hiring broke in a way you can measure. The average open role now gets more than 300 applications, up from about 100 in 2021. AI made applying almost free. The number of people who can read applications did not change.

Applying to more things faster is what caused this, so Career Ops does the opposite. It does not write or submit anything. It cuts down how many roles are worth your time.

It watches 87 job boards and surfaces only what fits a written profile. Fit is pass or fail: you clear the bar for a role or you do not. Among the ones you clear it ranks by how recently each was posted, because a req with 300 applicants gets read in arrival order. Pay band, employee sentiment and your history with that company are attached before you open a posting, and status comes from your mail, not from you.

It does not fix the real problem, and nothing on the candidate's side can: the bottleneck is how many humans can read. The hard bugs were all the same, software stating something untrue with total confidence and being believed. Most of the work is teaching it to say when it does not know.

Application volume: Ashby, 100M+ submissions. Interview-rate decline: ZipRecruiter 2026 talent acquisition survey.

Cost & forecasting Making a cost number travel, and arrive in time to matter

Service operations · method

Unit cost used to arrive from finance late in the year, when the only lever left was people. It became a number three functions signed and saw every month.

How it went

Before

Forecasts were badly wrong, teams hired against them, and then either shrink went up or they were overstaffed and had to cut. Cost reporting arrived from finance late in the year, by which point the only levers left were people. Engineering and operations barely spoke, even though operators depended on engineering delivery to hit their numbers.

One number everyone signs

Operators quantify their own initiatives in their own metrics. Workforce management converts that to FTE. Finance converts FTE to unit cost against the annual goal. Every input is cross-examined and signed by all three, and by engineering where delivery is a dependency. That gives you a gap to target at the start of the year rather than a discovery once the year is nearly spent.

The part that was missing

Execution. Finance publishes unit cost monthly to the operators who own hiring. A joint review puts two previously separate orgs in one room against one goal, so they can trade off against each other, one team carrying another's miss. It ties into demand modelling, which sits upstream of every staffing decision and is the largest single driver of cost.

Program governance Building a PMO for a deal that had not closed yet

Accenture · $26.5B telecom merger · 2018 – 2020

Several hundred people kept building through months of regulatory hold, on a deal that might not have survived review at all.

How it went

Nothing was broken here. Nothing existed yet. The client was standing up a PMO ahead of the merger and my team was on the ground floor, building the machinery that the next five years of integration work would run on.

What the deal did to the work

Terms were renegotiated several times with the FCC, the DOJ and the businesses, and the whole programme sat on hold for months. Until approval, there was a hard legal limit on what teams could do: design, build and test against synthetic data, never production data from the other party. Once initiatives had gone as far as the law allowed, engineers and PMs were reallocated across the wider Product and Technology org, and the designs sat.

And it was not a scheduled pause. At several points it was genuinely unclear whether the deal would survive review at all, which is a different job from running a programme with a known end state. You are building something that might never launch, and several hundred people still have to work on it like it will.

Why a pause is expensive

Months passed and the world moved underneath the designs. A call to an API that had since been deprecated. A data feed from a vendor team that had been repurposed. So before anyone restarted, the designs went back through a joint architecture review board: 52 projects reassessed over three months, high and low level designs re-checked against what was now true, with more than 150 project managers, senior managers, directors and architects pulled through it.

Those flaws would have surfaced anyway, in unit testing and BVT, at far higher cost. An estimated $2M of remediation avoided. These are easy to spot in a design review and expensive to find in testing.

Sixteen VPs and no authority over any of them

Data. I built the reporting that showed where work was actually stuck and whose team it was sitting on. Nobody had that view before. Those reports went up to the CIO, and that is what did the work: once a VP knows the row with their name on it is read two levels above them, they push each other and nobody has to be chased.

What went wrong

Mid-quarter, the client switched licensing from Tableau to Power BI and asked me to port every report across. I had never used Power BI, and it is different enough from Tableau in both capability and interface that it was not a translation job. I learned it on the fly and got the reporting back up.

Go to market Making an opportunity sellable

Google · publisher partnerships

Sellers already had the list. What they could not do was tell a publisher what it was worth, so it kept losing to pitches that arrived with a number.

How it went

Sellers already had the list. That was never the constraint. What they could not do was tell a publisher what the opportunity was worth, so it kept losing to pitches that arrived with a number attached.

Measure it, do not argue it

Impact Experiments, the incrementality framework inside Ad Manager 360, produced a measured figure per publisher rather than an estimate. A publisher will argue with a projection. They will not argue with their own incrementality result.

Between the people who had the evidence and the people who needed it

The measurement sat with product and pGTM. The publisher relationships sat with sales. Neither group was going to build the other's artefact. I ran the publisher meetings, took what came out of them back to product, and built the case study jointly with them, so it carried product's evidence in language a seller could put straight into a pitch. Sales stopped having to translate it, and started opening conversations they had been skipping.

One number, three places it had never been

Into the internal performance view, so sellers could finally book credit for the work. Into the client conversation, as a figure a publisher would accept. And onto a leaderboard, because I built the custom CRM and product views that did not exist and turned pitching it into a contest.

I timed the launch to the week the case study cleared legal and client review, so sellers got the proof they had been asking for and a reason to use it in the same week. Pipeline movement on that opportunity ran 256% against prior quarters.

What I got wrong

Early on I owned a report that a lot of people planned from. One of its inputs quietly stopped refreshing. Not broken, just stale, and it kept publishing every week with exactly the same confidence as always. It took me far longer than it should have to catch, because it was my report and I trusted it.

Nobody questions a system that looks authoritative, and the person who built it questions it least. That is the real risk in this job. A dashboard that breaks gets noticed the same afternoon. One that is quietly wrong gets planned against for a quarter, and by the time it surfaces people have made hiring and budget decisions on it.

So I spend more time now on how a system says it is not sure than on how it looks when it is right.
